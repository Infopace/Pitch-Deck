# How Investors Read Pitch Decks: Design, Narrative, Credibility Rules and Red Flags (as of Oct 2026)

Research date: 2026-10-27. Every data point is dated. Old studies (pre-2022) are flagged **[OLD]**. Vendor-sourced data (DocSend/Dropbox, Papermark, Storydoc) is flagged **[VENDOR]**: those firms sell deck-sharing tools and their samples are self-selected users of their own platforms.

Source-access note: docsend.com and fastcompany.com returned HTTP 403 to direct fetches. DocSend figures below come from DocSend press releases (PR Newswire / StockTitan), TechCrunch write-ups of DocSend data, the Dropbox-hosted DocSend design-guide landing page, and secondary blogs (flagged as secondary). The Fast Company column was read through its Fast Company Middle East mirror.

## 1. Investor reading behaviour: time per deck and per slide, which slides get attention, the 2015–2026 trend, and what predicts a follow-up

### Takeaway
On a cold read, investors spend about 2.5–4 minutes per deck view. DocSend's measured average fell to an all-time low of 2:24 in Q4 2023 and has not been reliably updated since September 2024. The cover/purpose slide and the team slide get the most attention, and later slides get about 15 seconds or less each. Decks that succeed open with a crisp company purpose (not the product), spread attention evenly, get re-read more often and longer, put traction early, and include competition and financials.

### Cited Findings

**Time per deck, as a timeline**
- **2015 [OLD][VENDOR]:** DocSend's 2015 study of 200+ decks found VCs spent an average of 3 min 44 s per deck. Many blogs still quote this as current. — [StartWise (secondary, citing DocSend 2015)](https://start-wise.io/blog/how-long-investors-spend-pitch-deck/); [TechCrunch, Heddleston, Apr 2019](https://techcrunch.com/2019/04/12/data-tells-us-that-investors-love-a-good-story)
- **2017→2018 [OLD][VENDOR]:** Deck views on DocSend grew 4x. Controlling for platform growth, investors viewed about 35% more decks in 2018 than in 2017. — [TechCrunch, Russ Heddleston (DocSend co-founder), Apr 12 2019](https://techcrunch.com/2019/04/12/data-tells-us-that-investors-love-a-good-story)
- **2021→2022 [VENDOR]:** Investors spent 24% less time on decks in 2022 than in 2021, leaving founders "just under three minutes" to earn a meeting. On decks that went on to fail, investors gave up after an average of 2 min 13 s. Sample: 320 decks. — [TechCrunch, Haje Jan Kamps, Sept 22 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/09/22/science-of-pitch-decks/amp)
- **Q4 2023 [VENDOR]:** Time per deck fell all through 2023 and hit an **all-time low of 2 min 24 s** in Q4 2023. Year-to-date time spent was down 3.8%. Pitch-deck interactions were up 6.7% YoY in Q4 2023 but down 4.8% for the full year. — [DocSend press release, PR Newswire, Jan 17 2024](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/docsend-2023-year-end-data-indicates-positive-fundraising-momentum-going-into-2024-302036345.html)
- **Q1 2024 [VENDOR]:** Average of about 2 min 30 s per deck. One weekly Startup Index reading in early 2024 was 2 min 18 s. — [SiliconANGLE, Apr 11 2024](https://siliconangle.com/2024/04/11/docsend-report-reveals-surprising-uptick-first-quarter-venture-capital-engagement/); weekly figure from search summary of [DocSend Startup Index](https://www.docsend.com/pitch-deck-metrics/) (page returned 403, so not verified)
- **Undated DocSend design guide [VENDOR]:** "The average investor time spent on your deck is two minutes and 42 seconds," and that time is decreasing. — [Dropbox DocSend deck-design report landing page](https://experience.dropbox.com/form/docsend/deck-design-report)
- **Possible end of DocSend's tracker:** One secondary source says DocSend's live pitch-deck metrics tracker has no data after September 2024, and that its last research press release is dated December 2024. — [search summary citing PitchGrade/StartWise blogs](https://pitchgrade.com/blog/what-investors-read-pitch-deck-docsend-data). An unverified secondary claim gives "1 min 56 s for seed decks, down 24% since 2021" (attributed to a "Spotlight on Startups 2026" reading of DocSend data). I could not trace it to a DocSend primary source, so treat it as unconfirmed.
- **Stage splits (secondary):** DocSend pages updated in 2026 reportedly give **4:10 for pre-seed** decks and **3:44 for seed** decks, a 58% seed completion rate, and a 1–2% pre-seed deck-to-meeting rate. These come through a blog and the observation windows are not stated. — [HummingDeck Pitch Deck Statistics 2026 (secondary)](https://hummingdeck.com/blog/pitch-deck-benchmarks-2026)
- **Papermark, about 3,000 decks [VENDOR]:** Average total view time is **3.2 min**. The first page gets **23 s**, and pages 2–10 get **about 15 s each**. The page's methodology is internally inconsistent: it gives n = 3,000 in one place and 2,239 in another, and dates the data both to Jan–Dec 2024 and to "collected in 2025/2026". — [Papermark Pitch Deck Metrics report](https://papermark.com/pitch-deck-metrics)
- **Papermark Fundraising Report 2026, covering 2024–2026 [VENDOR]:**
  - Sample: 24,541 decks, 358,672 investor views, 15.2M page-level data points.
  - **Average time per view: 4.0 min.** 16% of views end within 10 seconds. Median total attention per deck across all its viewers is 18 min, from a median of 11 unique investors per deck.
  - The team slide is the most-read slide at 5.7 s per view. The cover gets about 45% more time than the median page.
  - Only 44% of investors reach the last slide.
  - The report also gives a "deck median dwell per slide 3.3 s", which is hard to reconcile with the 4.0 min per view and may be measured differently. Flagged.
  - — [Papermark Fundraising Report](https://www.papermark.com/fundraising-report)
- **Storydoc, 1.3M+ sessions [VENDOR, secondary, unverified]:** 31% of sessions end within 10 seconds. The team slide takes 43% of reading time. Sessions that reach slide 4 complete 82% of the time. Engagement declines after 18 slides. These numbers come only through a secondary blog. I could not find the Storydoc report itself, and its data covers interactive web decks rather than PDFs. — [HummingDeck (secondary)](https://hummingdeck.com/blog/pitch-deck-benchmarks-2026)

**Which slides get attention**
- **2024 vs 2023 [VENDOR]:** Investors spent **40% more time on seed Team slides** and **30% more on pre-seed Team slides**. They spent **19% less on pre-seed Market Size** and **48% less on seed Competition**. Deck engagement hit record highs but "did not translate into more dollars." Sample: 400+ pre-seed and seed startups. — [DocSend press release, Dec 18 2024 (via StockTitan)](https://www.stocktitan.net/news/DBX/v-cs-prioritize-people-in-an-ai-heavy-landscape-according-to-new-z3rqvcox6orq.html)
- **2022 [VENDOR]:** Product and business model sections got the most attention, although time on them nearly halved at pre-seed. Company Purpose rose to third place and works as a "gatekeeper". — [TechCrunch, Sept 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/09/22/science-of-pitch-decks/amp)
- **Undated DocSend design guide [VENDOR]:** "VCs spent 88% more time on the competition section." — [Dropbox DocSend design report page](https://experience.dropbox.com/form/docsend/deck-design-report). This is directionally at odds with the 2024 finding of 48% less time on seed Competition slides. The two figures probably come from different years and stages.
- **Stated decision criteria:** In a survey of 885 institutional VCs at 681 firms (Nov 2015–Mar 2016, published JFE 2020), the management team was the factor most often named as most important. 95% of firms called the team important. The share naming it the single most important factor is reported as 47% by one source and "over 55%" by another. **[OLD]** — [Harvard Law School Forum, Gompers et al., Aug 2019](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2019/08/20/how-do-venture-capitalists-make-decisions); [NBER Digest, Dec 2016](https://www.nber.org/digest/dec16/how-do-venture-capitalists-make-decisions)

**What predicts a follow-up or a raise**
- **Successful vs failed decks [OLD][VENDOR]:**
  - Successful decks open with company purpose. Investors spent about **22 s** on the purpose slide in successful decks, against about **61 s** on the opening product page in failed decks, which was 20% of total deck time.
  - In failed decks, 56% of time went to the product, financials and team slides. In successful decks that share was 26%, and "no single slide drew significantly more attention".
  - Successful decks averaged **20 pages**, failed decks 17.
  - Sequoia's template "most closely matches the sequence" of successful decks.
  - — [TechCrunch, Heddleston, Apr 2019](https://techcrunch.com/2019/04/12/data-tells-us-that-investors-love-a-good-story)
- **Repeat visits [OLD][VENDOR]:** Successful decks are revisited about 2.3x more than failed ones. This comes from a search snippet describing a DocSend report reprinted on TechCrunch, and I did not verify it on the page. — [TechCrunch 2019](https://techcrunch.com/2019/04/12/why-pitching-your-product-will-kill-your-fundraising/amp/)
- **2022 [VENDOR]:**
  - The Team slide appeared in 100% of decks, successful or not.
  - Unsuccessful decks often lacked a competition slide.
  - About a quarter of decks included financials, and none of the unsuccessful decks did.
  - — [TechCrunch, Sept 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/09/22/science-of-pitch-decks/amp)
- **2024–2026 [VENDOR]:**
  - "Closed-round" decks got a **36 min** median total reading time, against 18 min for the typical deck, and **37% more time per page in the back half**.
  - Putting **traction in the first three slides** was associated with **20% more views**.
  - Papermark says deck order was not tested against outcomes, so causation is not established.
  - — [Papermark Fundraising Report](https://www.papermark.com/fundraising-report)
- **Time is not the same as interest [VENDOR]:** DocSend notes that "more time spent on a deck doesn't necessarily correlate with more funding." — [DocSend design report page](https://experience.dropbox.com/form/docsend/deck-design-report)

### Inferences
- Plan for about **2.5–4 minutes and about 15 s per later slide**. The cover, purpose and first 3–4 slides have to carry the whole thesis.
- Time per view is not a quality metric. Repeat views, the number of unique viewers (a sign the deck is being forwarded inside a firm), and time on the back half are better signals of investor interest. An analytics feature in iPreneur should surface those.
- Team attention is rising (2024 DocSend, Papermark 2026). The generator should make the team slide specific: earned expertise and founder–problem fit, not a grid of logos.
- No high-quality, non-vendor, peer-reviewed dataset on deck-reading behaviour after 2022 turned up. All the quantitative data comes from deck-sharing vendors.

### Gaps
- I found no verified DocSend 2025 or 2026 deck-metrics report. The DocSend tracker may have stopped after Sept 2024.
- The Brex, Carta, Visible.vc and Pitch.com research named in the brief returned nothing usable on reading time in the time available.
- There is no robust, non-vendor data linking specific slide features to meeting conversion.
- **Mobile viewing:** A DocSend seed-deck study reportedly found 12% of investors read decks on their phones. This came only from a search snippet and the [DocSend blog](https://www.docsend.com/blog/how-to-find-out-if-youve-created-a-successful-pitch-deck/) returned 403, so it is unverified and its date is unknown.

## 2. Design rules: fonts, words, one idea per slide, charts, screenshots, contrast, hierarchy, templates, mobile/PDF, file format, action titles

### Takeaway
The authorities agree:
- One idea per slide.
- Large type with high contrast.
- Key text at the top.
- Every chart and slide carries an explicit takeaway caption or headline.
- Little text, no ornament.

Note the conflict between "presented" decks (Kawasaki's minimum 30 pt, 10 slides) and "send-ahead/read" decks. Successful send-ahead decks in DocSend data ran about 19–20 pages and are read without a narrator.

### Cited Findings
- **Kawasaki 10/20/30 rule:** 10 slides, 20 minutes, "no font smaller than thirty points." His ten slides: problem, solution, business model, underlying magic/technology, marketing and sales, competition, team, projections and milestones, status and timeline, summary and call to action. **[OLD; original ~2005 blog]** The sources below are secondary. — [AhaSlides summary](https://ahaslides.com/blog/10-20-30-rule-presentations); [Alexander Jarvis summary](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/guy-kawasaki-10-20-30-rule-for-founders-startup-business-pitch-deck/)
- **YC, Kevin Hale (Nov 15 2015) [OLD but still YC canon]:** "Make it legible. Make it simple. Make it obvious." — [YC Blog](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)
  - **Legible:** readable by "old people sitting in the back row with bad eyesight". Use large type, bold text, a simple font, good contrast with the background, and no light-grey text. Place key text at the **top**, because it is easier to read.
  - **Simple:** "A simple slide… expresses one idea."
  - **Obvious:** the "stranger test". Show a slide to someone; if they don't immediately say your idea, "you lose."
  - **Charts:** write the takeaway out so the viewer "barely ha[s] to look at the graph at all." Avoid complex diagrams, which he calls "little mazes for ideas". Photos without titles or captions distract.
  - **Explaining the product:** a bulleted list of steps often works better than screenshots.
- **YC seed deck template:** deliberately plain. Its guidance includes "nobody cares about your advisors." This comes via search summary and was not fetched directly. — [YC Library, How to build your seed round pitch deck](https://ycombinator.com/library/2u-how-to-build-your-seed-round-pitch-deck)
- **Duarte Glance Test (HBR 2012) [OLD, still standard]:** An audience should grasp a slide's point in **3 seconds or less**, because "audiences can't read text-heavy slides and listen at the same time". Slides work like billboards. Use a single point per slide, contrast and whitespace. — [Duarte, The Glance Test](https://www.duarte.com/resources/guides-tools/the-glance-test/); [Small Wars Journal reprint](https://smallwarsjournal.com/2012/10/22/do-your-slides-pass-the-glance-test/)
- **Action titles / Pyramid Principle (consulting practice):**
  - Titles state the conclusion as a full sentence, not a topic label like "Q3 Revenue".
  - **Horizontal logic:** reading only the titles in sequence should tell the whole story.
  - **Vertical logic:** the body of each slide supports its title.
  - Convention is 15 words or fewer, at most two lines, active voice.
  - The method is attributed to Barbara Minto at McKinsey.
  - These are practitioner conventions from secondary guides, not an official McKinsey document. — [Deckary consulting slide guide](https://deckary.com/blog/consulting-quality-slides); [Piktochart McKinsey-style guide](https://piktochart.com/blog/mckinsey-style-presentation)
- **A tell of AI-made decks (Aug 2026):** Leslie Feinzaig, an early-stage VC, says AI-built decks look polished but share the same slides and social-media-style phrasing, and their **slide titles are fragments**. — [Fast Company (mirror), Aug 20 2026](https://fastcompanyme.com/?p=98594); original [fastcompany.com](https://www.fastcompany.com/91627159/vc-pitch-decks)
- **Density, named VCs (Aug 2025):** — [Vestbee, Aug 7 2025](https://vestbee.com/insights/articles/most-common-pitch-deck-mistakes-according-to-v-cs)
  - Peter Kromer (Day One Capital): "Less is more. 10-15 slides max."
  - Matous Palecek (J&T Ventures): "Founders often treat decks like investment-grade documents. But they're sales tools and nobody likes complicated ads."
- **Deck length data [VENDOR]:**
  - **Papermark (about 3,000 decks):** 9–16 pages is the most common band at 49%, then 17–24. — [Papermark metrics](https://papermark.com/pitch-deck-metrics)
  - **Papermark 2026 report:** 9–16 pages is 46% of decks. 12-page decks average 34 views with about half reaching the end. Completion drops about **20 points going from 8 to 16 pages**, and 16–18-page decks complete at about 40%. Even so, 9–24-page decks got 27 views versus 17 for decks under 9 pages. — [Papermark Fundraising Report](https://www.papermark.com/fundraising-report)
  - **DocSend:** successful decks averaged 20 pages vs 17 for failed decks **[OLD]** ([TechCrunch 2019](https://techcrunch.com/2019/04/12/data-tells-us-that-investors-love-a-good-story)). DocSend's seed recommendation is reportedly 19–20 pages ([HummingDeck, secondary](https://hummingdeck.com/blog/pitch-deck-benchmarks-2026)).
  - **Storydoc:** engagement declines after 18 slides (secondary, unverified; same HummingDeck source).
- **Front-loading [VENDOR]:** Papermark recommends stating the value proposition on page 1 and putting critical information in the first 3–4 pages, because later pages get about 15 s each. — [Papermark metrics](https://papermark.com/pitch-deck-metrics)
- **Words per slide:** DocSend says it identified "nine key factors, from words per page to how companies present their financials", but the thresholds weren't visible because the page returned 403. — [DocSend blog](https://www.docsend.com/blog/how-to-find-out-if-youve-created-a-successful-pitch-deck/)
- **File size and format, one named VC (Alexander Jarvis, post c. 2023):** — [Alexander Jarvis](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/whats-a-good-file-size-for-sending-a-pitch-deck-around/)
  - "In the perfect world, I would like the file size to be 3mb"; in practice 5–8 MB; "If I see 8mb or more, then there is a problem."
  - Send a compressed **PDF**, not PPT/Keynote. Share the PPT only after a term sheet.
  - About 10 MB is the safe attachment limit.

### Inferences
Generator rules these sources support:
- **(a) Action titles.** Every content slide gets a full-sentence title of 15 words or fewer that states the takeaway. This also counters the "fragment titles" AI tell Feinzaig describes.
- **(b) Horizontal-logic check.** The titles alone should read as the pitch.
- **(c) One idea per slide.** Charts carry a caption stating the conclusion, for example "Revenue grew 3.2x in 12 months".
- **(d) Type and contrast.**
  - Presented mode: body text of 24 pt or more, and 30 pt or more where possible (Kawasaki).
  - Send-ahead/read mode: smaller text is allowed (about 14–18 pt at 16:9), but keep high contrast and no light-grey body text.
  - In both modes, the title goes at the top.
- **(e) Front-load.** Purpose or value proposition on the cover or slide 2, and traction within the first 3 slides when it exists.
- **(f) Length.** Keep the core narrative to about 10–20 slides and put the rest in a labelled appendix.

**Note on the 20–30-slide target:** The evidence does not support a 20–30-slide main body. Completion and attention fall after about 12–18 slides. If iPreneur's product requirement is 20–30 total slides, the evidence-based approach is a core deck of about 12–18 slides plus an appendix (detailed financials, unit economics, customer case studies, sources, cap table, roadmap). Export the PDF compressed to 8 MB or less, ideally about 3–5 MB.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous study isolating font size, colour palette, serif vs sans, or image use against funding outcomes. DocSend's "nine factors" detail was inaccessible.
- I found no verified, recent data on what share of decks are read on mobile. The 12% DocSend figure is unverified.
- I found no data comparing outcomes for Pitch.com or Gamma web links against PDFs.
- I did not retrieve primary text from Tufte or Garr Reynolds. Data-viz guidance here rests on Hale's YC post and the consulting action-title conventions.

## 3. Narrative: frameworks, "why now", earned secret, opening hooks, the first 3 slides, and handling weaknesses

### Takeaway
Use the Sequoia-style order: purpose, problem, solution/insight, **why now**, market, competition, business model, team, financials, vision. It is the sequence DocSend found closest to successful decks. Open with a one-sentence company purpose, not a product demo. Make the timing argument explicit, and state the founder's non-obvious insight plainly. Investors read a deck to decide whether to take a meeting, not to learn everything, so it should be compelling rather than comprehensive.

### Cited Findings
- **Sequoia "Writing a Business Plan":** — [Sequoia Capital](https://sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan) (via search summary)
  - **Order:** company purpose (a single declarative sentence), problem, solution (your core insight, and why the value proposition is compelling and durable), **why now**, market potential, competition (direct and indirect, plus a plan to win), business model, team, financials, vision.
  - The purpose statement is hard to write because it is "easy to get caught up listing features instead of communicating your mission."
  - On why now: the best companies almost always have a clear answer, because a solution that seems obvious would already exist if the timing had been right.
  - TAM/SAM/SOM is **not** in Sequoia's own text. It was added in derivative templates.
- **Purpose first beats product first [OLD][VENDOR]:** Successful decks open with company purpose. Opening with the product correlated with failure. — [TechCrunch, Heddleston 2019](https://techcrunch.com/2019/04/12/data-tells-us-that-investors-love-a-good-story)
- **Purpose as gatekeeper [VENDOR]:** Company Purpose attention "really shot up" in 2022, and investors use it as a gatekeeper. It is usually one sentence on slide 1 or 2. — [TechCrunch 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/09/22/science-of-pitch-decks/amp)
- **DocSend narrative flow:** "Problem; Solution; Why now?" — [DocSend design report page](https://experience.dropbox.com/form/docsend/deck-design-report). DocSend's why-now guidance reportedly places it between Problem and Solution, and warns that many "Why Now" slides are really just "Why" slides. This comes via search summary; the page returned 403. — [DocSend blog: Why now slide](https://www.docsend.com/blog/why-now-slide)
- **Secrets and why now / why us:** A Thiel-derived framing treats the pitch as proving a "secret", and says it needs clear answers to "Why Now" and "Why Us", without which a pitch is "merely an observation of a trend". This is secondary, and I found no authoritative primary definition of "earned secret". — [search summary of Skillselion pitch-deck skill](https://skillselion.com/skills/joellewis/skill-library/pitch-deck)
- **Hale's memorability point:** listeners remember only a few reasons a company is great, so pick the 5–7 most important ideas. "If you just communicate your points clearly, you'll do better than 99% of startups." — [YC Blog, Hale 2015](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)
- **Leslie Feinzaig (Fast Company, Aug 20 2026):** — [Fast Company (mirror)](https://fastcompanyme.com/?p=98594)
  - "The goal of a pitch deck is not to be comprehensive, it's to be compelling."
  - Her team reviews decks only to decide whether to take a meeting: "99 times out of 100, we pass."
  - The deck must answer why this, why you, why now. It "doesn't even need to be a deck"; a short memo can work.
- **Brian Kerns (Unventure Capital / SWAN Impact Network; VentureWell, Sept 16 2026):** "Really good founders tell you a story," often a personal one. AI "is not going to reach into your soul." — [VentureWell](https://venturewell.org/blog/pitch-decks-artificial-intelligence-ai/)
- **Handling weaknesses and being candid about stage (Aug 2025):** — [Vestbee](https://vestbee.com/insights/articles/most-common-pitch-deck-mistakes-according-to-v-cs)
  - Kseniia Ocheredko (N1 Fund): "A deck that reads like all the answers are already known makes me nervous," and "Good storytelling matters, but not when it masks weak fundamentals."
  - Serhiy Tokarev (Roosh Ventures): founders sometimes leave out the traction slide "because they think only revenue matters". Show non-revenue traction instead.
  - Radoslav Cuha (PurposeTech): if the team "lacks deep insight or real experience with the problem, it shows in flawed assumptions."
- **Language:** Vestbee's editor advises founders not to be "scared of putting in your pitch some weird, industry-specific words your customers use". This is editorial opinion, not an investor's. — [Vestbee, Jun 10 2026](https://vestbee.com/insights/articles/pitch-decks-in-the-ai-era)

### Inferences
- **First three slides:**
  - Slide 1: cover with a one-line purpose or value proposition.
  - Slide 2: problem, with a concrete customer pain and how it is solved today.
  - Slide 3: either the insight/solution or traction (when the startup has it), because Papermark associates early traction with 20% more views.
- **Why now** is a required slide or section, with specific dated enabling changes: technology cost curves, regulation, behaviour shifts. A generic "the market is growing" should fail validation.
- **Insight / earned secret:** the generator should prompt the founder for one non-obvious belief they hold from direct experience, and refuse to fill it with generic text.
- **Weaknesses:** state risks and open questions candidly, for example in a "what we're testing next" or "key risks and mitigations" slide or appendix. Investors penalise decks that "know all the answers".

### Gaps
- I found no quantitative study isolating opening hooks, such as a story or statistic versus a purpose statement, beyond the DocSend purpose-versus-product finding.
- I did not retrieve primary Nancy Duarte material on narrative structure (her "what is / what could be" sparkline).
- I found no data on how decks should present weaknesses.

## 4. Credibility: unsourced numbers, inflated TAM, vanity metrics, AI-hallucinated statistics, "no competitors", buzzwords, and how to cite

### Takeaway
Investors read unsourced or top-down TAM figures, "no competitors" claims, buzzwords and unexplained hockey sticks as signs of shallow homework. Fabricated or hallucinated citations are the kind of error diligence catches, and they end deals. The evidence here is mostly named-VC opinion and practitioner consensus, not measured data.

### Cited Findings
- **"No competitors":**
  - Henrik Neninger (Lead Ventures): "Saying that 'we do not have competitors in this field' is an absolute red flag." — [Vestbee, Aug 2025](https://vestbee.com/insights/articles/most-common-pitch-deck-mistakes-according-to-v-cs)
  - Filip Bogdziun (Hard2Beat): "Claims of 'no competitors' only highlight a lack of homework." — same source
  - Andrew Grey (Tilia Impact Ventures): "Founders often fail to grasp the true nature of competition in their market." — same source
  - Dave Berkus: the claim "has killed more investment deals than almost any other statement." — via [search summary of Alejandro Cremades piece](https://alejandrocremades.com/why-pitch-decks-fail-decoding-investor-interpretation-of-founder-signals/)
  - Wallin & Baltaxe, angel-investing book: claiming no competition may signal the founder doesn't understand customers or market. Their example is that the first car's rivals were horses, trains, bicycles and walking. — [Holloway, Diligence on Competition](https://www.holloway.com/g/angel-investing/sections/diligence-on-competition)
  - DocSend 2022: unsuccessful decks often lacked a competition slide. — [TechCrunch 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/09/22/science-of-pitch-decks/amp)
- **TAM:**
  - Filip Bogdziun (Hard2Beat): "Many decks replace real market analysis with generic Venn diagrams and random TAM/SAM/SOM numbers." — [Vestbee, Aug 2025](https://vestbee.com/insights/articles/most-common-pitch-deck-mistakes-according-to-v-cs)
  - Practitioner consensus: investors often assume a large unsourced number was "a superficial effort made solely for the pitch deck". "If you cannot name the research firm, remove the number." Top-down TAM from a global report "tells the investor nothing about your business", so use bottom-up sizing (customer count × price). Stale sources, such as citing Gartner 2022 in 2026, suggest the deck was copy-pasted. These are advisor and vendor blogs. — [Equidam](https://www.equidam.com/benchmark-tam-startup-fundraising/); [Pitch Deck Guide](https://pitchdeckguide.com/tam-sam-som-pitch-deck/); [startupfundraising.com, citing sources](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/how-many-sources-can-i-add-to-one-pitch-deck-slide)
  - DocSend 2024: investors spent 19% **less** time on pre-seed Market Size slides, which suggests TAM is becoming a low-attention hygiene slide. — [DocSend Dec 2024](https://www.stocktitan.net/news/DBX/v-cs-prioritize-people-in-an-ai-heavy-landscape-according-to-new-z3rqvcox6orq.html)
- **Buzzwords and vagueness:**
  - Peter Kromer (Day One Capital): "Don't start with buzzwords and vague slogans." — [Vestbee, Aug 2025](https://vestbee.com/insights/articles/most-common-pitch-deck-mistakes-according-to-v-cs)
  - Magdalena Chalas (Elevator Ventures): include "round size, timeline, and how the funds will be used," plus the product's features, business model and pricing. — same source
- **Hallucinated or fabricated data:**
  - Axios showed ChatGPT producing a detailed, apparently invented "investor deck" for Wealthfront's confidential IPO filing, complete with metrics and bankers. — [Axios Pro, Jul 2 2025](https://www.axios.com/pro/fintech-deals/2025/07/02/chatgpt-hallucinations-wealthfront-ipo)
  - GPTZero reportedly found about 60% of references in a Dec 2025 EY advisory report appeared hallucinated, including a non-existent McKinsey report. This was reported secondhand; it shows hallucinated citations reaching professional documents. — [Development Corporate](https://developmentcorporate.com/corporate-development/ai-hallucinations-in-consulting-reports-are-now-an-enterprise-due-diligence-crisis/)
  - A vendor anecdote describes a fabricated market-sizing citation killing a $250K round. It comes from a fact-checking tool vendor and is unverified. — [Talkory](https://www.talkory.ai/blog/chatgpt-citation-killed-250k-investment)
- **AI-written financial models:** Investors on Searchfunder say AI-written Excel models are flagged "even more strongly" than decks. — [Searchfunder investor post, c. 2026](https://searchfunder.com/post/investor-perspective-ai-generated-pitch-decks-really-hurt-your-credibility)
- **Accountability:** "You're accountable. AI isn't." Founders must be able to defend every claim in diligence. — [Brian Kerns via VentureWell, Sept 2026](https://venturewell.org/blog/pitch-decks-artificial-intelligence-ai/)

### Inferences
Generator rules:
- **(a) Source every number.** Every market, statistic or third-party number carries a source (publisher plus year) in a slide footnote, about 8–10 pt, bottom-left. A full "Sources" appendix slide lists URLs.
- **(b) Block unsourced numbers.** Unsourced figures get a visible "[source needed]" flag, or the user must mark them as "founder estimate / internal data".
- **(c) No invented sources.** Never fabricate a source. If the LLM can't verify a figure, it must not output a precise number with a citation.
- **(d) Freshness.** Warn when a source is more than about 2 years old.
- **(e) TAM method.** Default to bottom-up market sizing with the arithmetic shown, and treat top-down as secondary.
- **(f) Competition is mandatory.** Include status quo and manual workarounds as competitors. Block "no competitors" phrasing.
- **(g) Buzzword lint.** Flag and penalise words like "revolutionary", "disruptive", "AI-powered platform" or "Uber for X" when they have no concrete specifics.
- **(h) Projections.** Show the stated assumptions behind any projection curve.
- **(i) Vanity metrics.** Show metrics with a denominator and time base (MoM growth, retention, paying customers) rather than raw cumulative sign-ups.

### Gaps
- I found no quantitative survey measuring how often investors check deck sources, or how often fabricated statistics are caught.
- I found no verified, named public case of a founder's AI-hallucinated deck statistic ending a round.
- I found no investor guidance specifically comparing footnotes with appendix citations. The footnote-plus-sources-appendix rule above is my inference from practitioner advice.
- Vanity-metric guidance from named VCs was not retrieved in this pass.

## 5. AI-generated decks: what VCs say (2024–2026), whether they detect or penalise them

### Takeaway
Named investors in 2025–2026 say they object to sameness and outsourced thinking, not to AI use itself. AI decks are described as easy to spot: polished but generic, with identical slide structures, social-media phrasing and fragment-style titles. A deck that merely looks AI-generated carries a stigma. Meanwhile investors increasingly use AI themselves to triage decks. No rigorous survey quantifies detection or penalty rates.

### Cited Findings
- **Leslie Feinzaig, early-stage VC (Fast Company, Aug 20 2026), "Why founders should never use AI-generated pitch decks":** — [Fast Company (mirror)](https://fastcompanyme.com/?p=98594); [original](https://www.fastcompany.com/91627159/vc-pitch-decks)
  - AI decks are visually polished but share the same slides, social-media-style phrasing and fragment slide titles.
  - "when you build your deck with AI, you sound exactly like everyone else."
  - Reading many AI decks in one sitting is "downright unbearable."
  - AI is fine for research, automation and polishing: "I do take issue with those who use AI to do the thinking and communicating for them."
- **Brian Kerns (Unventure Capital / SWAN Impact Network; VentureWell, Sept 16 2026):** — [VentureWell](https://venturewell.org/blog/pitch-decks-artificial-intelligence-ai/)
  - Assumes deal-room documents are likely AI-engineered, and that decks are converging on "the exact same optimized pitch deck".
  - Sameness predates AI; AI "just made it free and instant."
  - "Often the decks seem to lack a soul."
  - Outsourcing critical thinking is a "100% fail".
  - Expects investors to require **disclosure of AI use** and likes founders showing their prompts.
- **Charles Hudson (Precursor Ventures):** AI-generated decks don't just risk inaccuracies, "they risk sameness." Reported secondhand by a journalist on Medium, undated (c. 2025–2026). — [Medium, A. Goldfisher](https://agoldfisher.medium.com/ai-is-killing-the-pitch-deck-and-that-might-be-a-good-thing-925562ae6f4c)
- **Searchfunder investors (Chicago Booth investor, with INSEAD and MIT investor replies; c. 2026, undated):** — [Searchfunder](https://searchfunder.com/post/investor-perspective-ai-generated-pitch-decks-really-hurt-your-credibility)
  - "You will get this stigma even if your deck just *looks* AI-generated."
  - An AI deck signals "I outsourced my thinking to AI."
  - "Everyone is able to tell AI generated answers from those you wrote yourself."
  - AI is acceptable for the executive-summary slide but undermines credibility elsewhere.
- **Vestbee editor Katarzyna Groszkowska (Jun 10 2026):** "AI-generated content makes it harder and harder to actually spot good, disruptive solutions"; "I don't think using AI is a bad thing." She is not an investor. — [Vestbee](https://vestbee.com/insights/articles/pitch-decks-in-the-ai-era)
- **Ruben Dominguez (The VC Corner, Mar 2026):** every recently funded seed founder he interviewed "used AI somewhere in their fundraising prep". He contrasts using AI to stress-test the story with pasting "write me a pitch deck" into ChatGPT. The post is paywalled; only the opening was visible. — [The VC Corner](https://www.thevccorner.com/p/ai-fundraising-prep-founders-tactical-playbook)
- **Investors using AI to screen:**
  - PitchBook/Web Summit 2025 survey of 116 investors: about one third use AI to summarise due-diligence materials, and about a quarter to identify deals. Secondary report; small sample. — [KoreaTechDesk](https://koreatechdesk.com/ai-venture-capital-screening-startup-due-diligence)
  - Affinity survey of about 300 private-capital dealmakers: 85% use AI to automate daily tasks, up from 76%. Secondary report. — [Seedforge](https://seedforge.com/blog/ai-in-venture-capital-deal-evaluation-2026)
  - Claims that EQT and Moonfire use AI to pre-screen decks appeared only in content-marketing blogs and were not verified.

### Inferences
- iPreneur's differentiation should be **"AI-assisted, founder-authored"**, not "AI-written". The generator should:
  - (a) force founder-specific inputs (personal origin story, customer quotes, real metrics, the specific insight) and refuse to fabricate them;
  - (b) avoid stock phrasing and generic slide titles, using full-sentence action titles with specifics;
  - (c) vary layouts and avoid an obvious default-template look, because looking AI-generated alone triggers stigma;
  - (d) flag generic content with a "sameness" lint, such as phrases common across decks or claims that have no numbers.
- Because investors use AI to screen, structured, clearly labelled slides carrying concrete numbers and sources may parse better in AI triage. This is speculative and I found no data on it.
- Kerns suggests a possible future norm of **AI-use disclosure**. Consider an optional "how this deck was prepared" note.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous survey (DocSend, PitchBook, NFX, etc.) measuring what share of VCs detect, penalise or ignore AI-generated decks.
- The TechCrunch, Business Insider, Sifted and The Information coverage requested in the brief was not surfaced by searches.
- I found no public statement from Hustle Fund/Elizabeth Yin, NFX, Point Nine or Tunguz on AI decks in this pass.

## 6. Most common reasons decks get rejected (red flags)

### Takeaway
Recurring red flags from named VCs (2025–2026), and from DocSend's successful-vs-failed comparisons, fall into three groups:
- **Substance:** no or weak competitive analysis, unsourced or generic TAM, an unclear purpose, product-first openings, a weak or ill-fitted team, no clear ask, missing traction.
- **Style:** bloat, buzzwords, density.
- **New for 2025–2026:** generic, AI-sounding sameness.

No large quantitative dataset of rejection reasons was found.

### Cited Findings
- **The base rate is high rejection:** "99 times out of 100, we pass." — [Leslie Feinzaig, Fast Company, Aug 2026](https://fastcompanyme.com/?p=98594). A DocSend-reported pre-seed deck-to-meeting rate of 1–2% comes via a secondary source. — [HummingDeck](https://hummingdeck.com/blog/pitch-deck-benchmarks-2026)
- **Abandonment timing [VENDOR]:** investors gave up on decks that failed after 2 min 13 s (2022). — [TechCrunch 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/09/22/science-of-pitch-decks/amp). 16% of Papermark views end within 10 seconds (2024–2026). — [Papermark Fundraising Report](https://www.papermark.com/fundraising-report)
- **Structural correlates of failure [VENDOR]:**
  - Product-first openings, and attention concentrated on product, financials and team (56% vs 26% in successful decks) **[OLD]**. — [TechCrunch 2019](https://techcrunch.com/2019/04/12/data-tells-us-that-investors-love-a-good-story)
  - A missing competition slide, and missing financials (2022). — [TechCrunch 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/09/22/science-of-pitch-decks/amp)
- **Named-VC red flags (Vestbee, Aug 7 2025):** — [Vestbee](https://vestbee.com/insights/articles/most-common-pitch-deck-mistakes-according-to-v-cs)
  - Too long: more than 10–15 slides (Kromer).
  - Buzzwords and slogans (Kromer).
  - Missing round size, timeline or use of funds (Chalas; Tokarev: "Every pitch should end with a clear, confident ask").
  - Generic TAM and Venn diagrams (Bogdziun).
  - "No competitors" (Bogdziun, Neninger).
  - Misreading the competition (Grey).
  - A team without deep problem experience (Cuha).
  - Storytelling that masks weak fundamentals (Ocheredko).
  - Missing traction (Tokarev).
  - Bogdziun also notes: "In pitch decks, it's rarely the technical or visual flaws that stand out." Substance problems dominate.
- **Advisors (no data):** hockey-stick projections without assumptions, vague asks, and vague traction without numbers. — [IdeaProof list](https://ideaproof.io/lists/startup-pitch-mistakes); [WaveUp](https://waveup.com/blog/pitch-deck-mistakes-and-how-to-avoid-them/)
- **AI sameness and outsourced thinking (2026)** are now named as reasons to pass. — [Feinzaig](https://fastcompanyme.com/?p=98594); [Kerns](https://venturewell.org/blog/pitch-decks-artificial-intelligence-ai/); [Searchfunder](https://searchfunder.com/post/investor-perspective-ai-generated-pitch-decks-really-hurt-your-credibility)

### Inferences
Suggested hard validation checks for the generator, each mapped to a red flag:
1. Purpose sentence on the cover or slide 2.
2. Problem before product.
3. Why-now slide.
4. Competition slide with at least 3 named alternatives, including the status quo.
5. Bottom-up TAM with sources.
6. Traction with real numbers, or an explicit "pre-traction milestones" slide.
7. Team slide with relevant experience.
8. An ask slide with amount, use of funds and the milestones the money buys.
9. Projections with assumptions.
10. Buzzword and sameness lint.
11. Core deck of about 20 slides or fewer, with the remainder in an appendix.

### Gaps
- I found no large-sample, non-vendor quantitative ranking of rejection reasons for 2024–2026. Most lists are VC opinion or advisor listicles.
- Hustle Fund, NFX and Point Nine specific red-flag lists were not retrieved in this pass.

## 7. Accessibility and practical delivery: size limits, tracking links, cold outreach, appendix, per-investor versioning

### Takeaway
Send a compressed PDF of about 3–8 MB, or an **ungated** tracked link. Many investors dislike email gates. Most founders use a single link for everyone, though per-investor links enable tracking. Engagement unfolds over weeks to months: a median of 7.9 hours to first open, and a median of about 6 months from first view to an announced round.

### Cited Findings
- **File size and format (Alexander Jarvis, VC; post c. 2023):** ideally 3 MB, typically 5–8 MB, and 8 MB or more is "a problem". Use a compressed PDF, not PPT/Keynote. "Always email your deck." Avoid WeTransfer-style services with strangers. About 10 MB is the safe attachment limit. — [Alexander Jarvis](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/whats-a-good-file-size-for-sending-a-pitch-deck-around/)
- **Email gates:**
  - Jarvis: "I do not want to enter my fricking email." He still "generally recommend[s] DocSend to founders" for the analytics. — same source
  - vcbeast: some investors skip DocSend links and ask for a PDF. — [vcbeast](https://vcbeast.com/docsend-alternatives)
  - DocSend's co-founder argues links give per-investor control, revocation and re-open alerts. Vendor view. — [DocSend blog](https://www.docsend.com/blog/why-vcs-should-love-docsend-links/)
- **What founders actually do [VENDOR]:** — [Papermark metrics](https://papermark.com/pitch-deck-metrics); [Papermark Fundraising Report](https://www.papermark.com/fundraising-report)
  - Papermark, about 3,000 decks: 77% enable an email gate; 91% enable view notifications; 70% use a single link; 2.1 links per deck on average.
  - Papermark 2026: 79% send one link to everyone; 92% enable alerts; only 18% allow download.
- **Open and follow-up timing (Papermark 2024–2026) [VENDOR]:** — [Papermark Fundraising Report](https://www.papermark.com/fundraising-report)
  - 34% of decks are opened within an hour; the median time to first open is 7.9 h; 24% are still unopened after 3 days.
  - 26.5% are reopened on a later day.
  - The median from first view to an announced round is 6 months; decks are read over an average window of 59 days.
  - About two-thirds of views come from outside the US.
- **Fundraise timelines [VENDOR]:**
  - 2022: 58% of startups raised in under 12 weeks; 90% in under 24 weeks. — [TechCrunch 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/09/22/science-of-pitch-decks/amp)
  - 2024: pre-seed raises averaged 12 weeks. — [DocSend Dec 2024](https://www.stocktitan.net/news/DBX/v-cs-prioritize-people-in-an-ai-heavy-landscape-according-to-new-z3rqvcox6orq.html)
  - **[OLD]:** most startups pitch 20–30 investors to close a round, and seed rounds take 24% longer than Series A. — [TechCrunch 2019](https://techcrunch.com/2019/04/12/data-tells-us-that-investors-love-a-good-story)
- **Video pitches [VENDOR]:** median length 4:33, 44% median watch-through, 31% drop in the first tenth. Small sample: 357 views of 29 videos. — [Papermark Fundraising Report](https://www.papermark.com/fundraising-report)
- **Alternative formats:** Feinzaig says a short memo or a strong email can replace a deck: "It doesn't even need to be a deck!" — [Fast Company (mirror), Aug 2026](https://fastcompanyme.com/?p=98594)

### Inferences
Product implications for iPreneur:
- **(a) Export.** Compressed PDF targeting 8 MB or less, plus an optional web link with **no email gate by default**.
- **(b) Per-investor links.** Optional, for tracking reopen and forward signals.
- **(c) Separate modes.** A "send-ahead" reading version that is self-explanatory with action titles, and a sparser "presenting" version (30 pt or more, fewer words).
- **(d) Appendix.** An auto-generated appendix holding detailed financials, sources, team bios and product screenshots, keeping the core deck short.
- **(e) Teaser.** A short teaser or email blurb generator for cold outreach.

### Gaps
- I found no data on accessibility for screen readers or colour-blind investors; no relevant investor sources surfaced.
- I found no quantitative data on cold-outreach response rates for attached PDFs versus links, or on per-investor deck customisation and its outcomes.
- I found no recent verified data on mobile reading share.
